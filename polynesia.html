How We Know Polynesian Languages Are a Family

Reading the Past Without Written Records

Schütz: looking back thousands of years for the origins of Polynesian languages, we find only the most durable clues.

  • Lapita pottery was first made in New Britain and Mussau around 1,400 BC, and its makers can be traced back another two thousand years to Taiwan
  • Languages ancestral to Polynesian were spoken during this long period of exchange, though no epics or chants mark the milestones
  • Researchers reconstruct earlier movements with archaeology, radiocarbon dating, DNA studies, and above all language — in complex relationship with people’s movements, which can cut off communication

Discuss: Why might language be the most important evidence for Polynesian origins, when we also have pottery, DNA, and radiocarbon dates?

Schütz, p. 97. The chapter notes that scholars’ estimates of the Austronesian family run 900-1200 languages because it is hard to draw the line between dialect and language, a callback to the Chuukic “language limit” from Tuesday.

Possible student answers to the Discuss (my synthesis, not Schütz’s claims; Schütz says only that language “still provides the most important data”). It’s fine to push back on “most important”: the strongest answer is that the lines of evidence complement each other.

  • Pottery and carbon dates tell us what and when, not who or how. Lapita sherds and radiocarbon dates date a site but say nothing about what people spoke or who they were related to. Language links people to people.
  • Pottery doesn’t last. Many Polynesian islands stopped making pottery, so the trail goes cold just where we most need it.
  • Language is carried by people, not objects. Words can’t be traded like pots, and a language is learned from other speakers, so it records real contact and descent. (Vocabulary can be borrowed, which is why linguists weight core vocabulary and sound correspondences.)
  • Sound correspondences are systematic. Regular patterns like k/t and h/f/wh are very hard to get by chance or borrowing, so they give a testable family tree.
  • Language can be compared across the whole family. We can line up 30+ languages across thousands of miles, which no single archaeological site allows.
  • DNA shows ancestry, not speech. Genes can show who mixed with whom, but not which language they spoke. Peoples sometimes shift languages and keep their genes, or the reverse.
  • No single source is enough. Each can mislead on its own. Early glottochronology dates and the Cook/Waimea impression of “one language” later in the deck are cautionary tales. The strongest conclusions come when language, archaeology, and DNA agree, and the live debates come when they don’t (see “Still Debated”).

Spices, Sailors, and Word Lists

  • Europeans first met Polynesian languages through the spice trade: the quest for cloves, mace, nutmeg, and pepper, controlled by Venetian merchants, drove ships to look for new routes
  • In 1615-16, Dutch partners Willem Schouten and Isaac Le Maire sailed to find a new route to the East Indies. Their crew had scurvy, and in May 1616 they reached islands near Vava’u in the Tonga group
  • Le Maire’s son Iacob collected 32 words from Tafahi and 118 words from East Futuna — the first written sample of a Polynesian language in Europe
  • The lists were useless for talking to other islanders: later explorers (Abel Tasman) tried them on Māori and Tongan with little success

Schütz, pp. 97-98. Point out the “voyages of discovery” framing: the discoveries were a byproduct of a commercial motive.

Reland Spots the Pattern (1706)

  • Dutch scholar Hadrian Reland compared the Tafahi/East Futuna words with Malay and saw similarities “too striking to be coincidental”
  • His idea: languages as far apart as Tonga and the Malay Peninsula and Madagascar belong to one family, later called Malayo-Polynesian, now a subgroup of Austronesian
  • Captain James Cook’s voyages (later 1700s) confirmed it. His naturalists published a comparative table of numerals for 39 languages showing the “affinity and extent of language”

Schütz, pp. 98-99. Reland proposed this more than 60 years before Cook; Cook’s data confirmed it.

What’s in the Name “Polynesia”?

  • Charles de Brosses coined the term in his 1756 Histoire, dividing the Pacific into three parts and naming the third “Polynesia,” from Greek polus ‘many’ + nēsos ‘island’
  • Even the name had a commercial motive: de Brosses wanted to suggest that the Pacific might hide “a large number of islands rich in spiceries”

Discuss: We use “Polynesia,” “Micronesia,” and “Melanesia” as if they were natural categories. What does it mean that they come from European explorers and mapmakers? (Callback to Tuesday: culture areas are defined by peoples and languages, not borders on a map.)

Schütz, p. 100. Ties back to the “What Is a Culture Area?” slide in the Micronesia deck. Tcherkézoff (2003) is the source Schütz cites for the de Brosses quotation.

Which Way Did They Sail?

  • For years the settlement of the Pacific was assumed to run west to east. Thor Heyerdahl argued the opposite: that Polynesia was settled from South America, and sailed the Kon-Tiki (1947) to show it could be done
  • He proved an east-to-west trip was possible, but evidence from several fields still pointed overwhelmingly to west-to-east migration
  • That doesn’t mean there was no contact with South America. Blust: the sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) in the Pacific before Columbus shows contact, but it is evidence of voyagers from the Pacific reaching South America, not the reverse
  • Even further back: isotope analysis of stone tools shows people crossing 100 miles of open water from the South China coast to the Penghu Islands in the Taiwan Strait, “the first stage of Austronesian seafaring” (Jiao, quoted in Krauss 2006)

Schütz, pp. 101-102. Good place to remind students that the same evidence base (archaeology, DNA, language) keeps revising the story.

The Polynesian Languages Today

Where, How Many, What?

Three simple-sounding questions:

  1. Where? Within the Polynesian triangle (over 2,000,000 square miles), plus the Outliers outside it
  2. How many? One scholar counts 53 speech communities grouped into 26 languages (Biggs 1971), but other counts differ, since the line between dialect and language is hazy
  3. What are they called? Clark (1979) lists 34 names

Schütz, pp. 101-102. The same dialect/language problem we hit with the Chuukic continuum on Tuesday.

Same Language, or Different?

  • In 1769 Captain Cook left Tahiti for Aotearoa with Tupaia, a Tahitian priest, adviser, and navigator. When they met the Māori, Tupaia called out “in the language of Otaheite” and the Māori seemed to understand
  • In 1778, Hawaiians at Waimea, Kaua’i, understood the Tahitian some of Cook’s crew spoke. Samwell wrote the language was “the same as that of Otaheite”
  • That gave rise to the idea that Polynesians spoke one language with dialects. Even missionary linguists like John Davies, who wrote a Tahitian dictionary, used the singular
  • But the “perfect understanding” was only the Europeans’ impression. Schütz: it was probably “a communication of simple ideas, enhanced by gestures”

Discuss: Why might first-time visitors have overestimated how well the languages were understood across islands?

Schütz, p. 103. Cook’s crew had reasons to hear similarity (they wanted trade and fresh supplies), and the dialect/language question is still unsettled. Note Schütz’s later point: the Waimea ease of understanding may actually be evidence for continued contact, or later settlement (see the When slide).

Sound Correspondences

Cognates aren’t identical; the sounds change in regular ways:

Hawaiian Māori Samoan Tongan
‘eye’ maka mata mata mata
‘house’ hale whare fale fale
‘sacred’ kapu tapu tapu tapu
  • Hawaiian k corresponds to t in Māori, Samoan, and Tongan
  • Hawaiian h corresponds to f in Samoan and Tongan, and wh in Māori

Try it: Hawaiian kāne ‘man’. What would you predict for Māori and Samoan?

Only ‘maka/mata’ is in Schütz (p. 105); the other two rows and the correspondences are my addition from general knowledge of Polynesian sound laws. Verify before class. ‘kāne’ → Māori tāne, Samoan tane (Tongan tangata is a different word). Connects to the regular sound change idea from the cognates unit in Week 5.

The Family Tree

Schütz Figure 3 (Clark 1979, based on Pawley 1966). Key points: (1) Tongan and Niuean branch off first; (2) Samoan sits with a group that includes most Outliers, so Nukuoro and Kapingamarangi are closer to Samoan than to Hawaiian; (3) Hawaiian is in East Polynesian, with Rapanui as the separate branch. Before Pawley (1966), scholars split Polynesian geographically into East vs. West, with Tongan and Samoan together as Western. Pawley’s grammatical innovations broke that up: a good example of language family evidence overriding geography, same lesson as Tuesday.

Reworking the Tree

  • Linguist Mary Walworth (2014) argues the Tahitic and Marquesic subgroups were “based on weak evidence” and removes them, using archaeology and linguistics together
  • Her revised tree puts most Eastern Polynesian languages (Hawaiian, Tahitian, Marquesan, Māori…) in one Central Eastern group with no finer internal grouping. Rapanui is the only branch that really developed in isolation
  • Why: most Eastern Polynesian languages seem to have developed in contact with each other, which matches archaeological evidence for rapid settlement and ongoing exchange

Discuss: If languages keep influencing each other after they split, how well does a tree describe them?

Schütz, pp. 106-107. Walworth’s evidence for a “wave” (contact) model rather than a clean branching tree. Zotero has Walworth 2014 if you want the original. Optional callback if you’ve covered the tree vs. wave models of language change earlier in the course (e.g., François, “Trees, Waves and Linkages”).

Still Debated: Where Did Eastern Polynesian Come From?

Two linguists, same question, different answers:

Walworth (2014): the languages grew together

  • New radiocarbon dates suggest eastern Polynesia was settled late and fast
  • Too little time for a neat branching tree; people kept trading and talking across islands
  • Shared features spread by contact, not just inheritance

Wilson (2021): look to the northwest

  • Eastern Polynesian’s closest relatives are tiny Outlier atolls (Takuu, Nukumanu, Luangiua, Nukeria) northeast of New Guinea, in the Bougainville and Solomon Islands area
  • Homeland: somewhere around the Phoenix, Line, and Marquesas Islands
  • Hawaiian may descend from its own northern branch, not from a Marquesan or Tahitian one

Discuss: Both use word comparisons. Why can smart people looking at the same words still disagree? What other evidence (archaeology, DNA, navigation) would you want?

Ties back to Back to Waimea and the settlement-date table: new dates + new linguistic models are still reshaping the story. Hawaiian’s closest relatives are an open question; there is no consensus. Keep it conceptual, no reconstruction details. The point for 100-level: linguistics is an active field, and “how closely related” depends on whether you model splitting (tree) or contact (wave). Walworth, M. 2014. “Eastern Polynesian: The Linguistic Evidence Revisited.” Oceanic Linguistics 53(2): 256-272 (uses Wilmshurst et al. 2011 radiocarbon dates). Wilson, W. When Was Hawai’i Settled?

The estimated date keeps moving:

Source Estimate
Glottochronology (Elbert 1953) A.D. 930-1300
Pawley & Green 1973 (based on Kirch) 9th century
Kirch 1985 by A.D. 300-400
Bellwood 1987 by A.D. 400, perhaps from the Marquesas
Hunt (2011) & Kahn et al. (2014) after A.D. 800-1000
  • Glottochronology assumes basic vocabulary changes at a roughly fixed rate. Too variable to trust alone (e.g., the Tahitian practice of pi’i, word replacement, changed vocabulary faster than expected)
  • Linguistic dates only become useful when checked against carbon-14 dating and oral genealogies

Schütz, pp. 108-110. Sources for each row as cited by Schütz. Carbon-14 dates cited: A.D. 818-1190; genealogy date A.D. 1050. Hunt (via Kubota 2011): Polynesians reached Samoa and Tonga about 800 B.C., then moved east again about A.D. 1100, settling Tahiti and the rest of eastern Polynesia between A.D. 1200 and 1300, helped by improved vessels and favorable El Niño winds.

Back to Waimea

Remember Cook’s crew understanding the Hawaiians at Waimea? A later settlement date changes how we read that story:

  • If Hawai’i was settled late, Tahitian and Hawaiian haven’t had long to drift apart
  • Or there was continued contact between Hawai’i and its relatives to the south. Schütz: current opinion dismisses the idea that Hawai’i was reached once and then stayed isolated
  • Origins: archaeology, linguistics, anthropology, and ethnobotany point to the Marquesas as the immediate homeland of the first settlers (Kirch), with Tahiti connections coming later, and possibly more than one voyage
  • DNA points the same way: a common lineage spread across the Pacific, which geneticist Rebecca Cann says could only happen with constant contact. In her words: “The Polynesians used the ocean as a superhighway”

Discuss: Hōkūle’a and Hawai’iloa have shown that long voyages work. How does that change what you thought “isolated islands” meant?

Schütz, pp. 109-110. Closing thought: modern technology (DNA, redated archaeological sites) is bringing earlier events into sharper focus, even as time moves them further away. Schütz also notes DNA evidence for occasional voyages east to South America, not just west to east.

Three Questions Linguists Ask

Comparing Polynesian languages pair by pair, scholars ask:

  1. How much do speakers of the two languages understand each other?
  2. How many basic words do they share?
  3. When did the languages split, when did speakers lose contact?

The third question needs both linguistic and non-linguistic evidence.

Exit ticket (3 min): Pick one of today’s three ways of measuring closeness (understanding, shared words, or sound correspondences). What does it tell us, and what does it miss?
