Four Kinds of Pacific Languages

Category Description
① Indigenous Native to the country or territory — what most of this course has focused on so far.
② Metropolitan The colonial or national language of government, education, and formal life — usually English, sometimes French, Spanish, or Indonesian.
③ Pidgin / Creole Contact languages like Bislama, Tok Pisin, Pijin — covered in depth Thursday.
④ Immigrant Languages brought by more recent arrivals. Lynch sets this aside in the chapter — keep it in mind for Fiji.

~4 min framing. New content from Ch.7 §2. Flag pidgin-creole is a quick callback only. End by flagging Fiji Hindi as a preview — technically “immigrant” by this framework, yet functions almost like a metropolitan language.

Vanuatu vs. Samoa

Team Vanuatu

  • ~100 indigenous languages, ~250,000 people
  • Jointly ruled by England & France until 1980 — both English and French are official
  • Bislama spoken by ~95% of the population
  • Typical ni-Vanuatu speaks 3+ languages: local language at home, Bislama in town, English or French at school/work
  • Port Vila: ~85% of the country’s languages have some speakers there

Team Samoa

  • 1 indigenous language: Samoan
  • Former German colony (to WWI), independent from New Zealand since 1962
  • Just two languages in daily use: Samoan and English
  • Samoan is the medium of instruction in primary school
  • English = formal/modern settings; Samoan = basically everywhere else

Fiji: The In-Between Case

  1. Fijian — actually a dialect chain; eastern/western and northern/southern speakers don’t always understand each other. A standardized eastern variety is used officially.
  2. Rotuman — spoken on Rotuma, part of Fiji but its own language, related to Fijian but distinct.
  3. Fiji Hindi — brought by Indian indentured laborers in the late 1800s.

~4 min. New territory. Introduce Fiji as the messy in-between case. Three-way split sets up the Fiji Hindi deep-dive next.

Fiji Hindi

~60,000

Indian indentured laborers (girmitiyas) brought to Fiji 1879–1916 on 5-year contracts to work sugarcane

~350,000

descendants in Fiji today — about 30% of the country’s population

“Fiji Baat”

the resulting language, mainly from Awadhi and Bhojpuri — now an official language of Fiji (as “Hindi”)

Language attitudes, again: Fiji Hindi is often looked down on by speakers of Standard Hindi/Urdu as “broken” or “inferior” — even though linguists are clear it’s a full, rule-governed language, not a degraded one. Same pattern we’ve seen with Pidgin/Creole attitudes.

~8 min. This is the expanded section — spend real time here.

Fiji Hindi: Girmit History

May 14, 1879

the ship Leonidas arrives at Levuka from Calcutta with the first 463 indentured laborers

37 years

1879–1916: the full span of Indian indentured emigration to Fiji, ~60,500+ people total

5-year contracts

“Girmit” is a mispronunciation of “agreement” — now the name for the whole era

Today: May 14 is observed as Girmit Day by Indo-Fijian communities in Fiji and the diaspora.

~5 min. Concrete historical anchor — a specific ship, date, and number of people rather than just aggregate stats.

Discussion: Fiji

  • Fiji has real internal diversity — the Fijian dialect chain plus Rotuman — that looks more like the Melanesian pattern.
  • But a standardized Fijian and English both function as unifying, public-life languages — more like the Polynesia/Micronesia pattern.
  • And then there’s Fiji Hindi — a whole additional layer neither pattern predicts, because it’s about 19th-century indentured labor migration, not indigenous settlement history.

Discuss: What does Fiji teach us about the limits of a two-category explanation?

~4 min discussion. Cold-call or pair-share.

The Telephone Tree

  1. Four communities, one ancestor — split into 4 groups. Everyone starts with: “The octopus stole my sandwich at the market.”
  2. Pass it down — telephone-style within each group, several rounds, no writing anything down.
  3. A later split — split each of the 4 groups in half again, run one more short round within each half.
  4. Compare all eight — write all 8 final versions on the board. Which resemble each other? Which drifted furthest?

~12-15 min. This models Lynch’s Figure 1 (branching diversification) experientially, no phonetics required.

Telephone Tree: Debrief

Notice: the two halves that split from the same group later usually still sound more alike than the more distantly split groups. That’s exactly the branching pattern behind real language families.

~50,000 yrs — settlement in parts of Melanesia (New Guinea region)

~3,000 yrs — oldest settlements in Polynesia/Micronesia

~1,000 yrs — since Hawaiʻi and New Zealand were first settled

~4 min debrief + bridge. More “rounds” = more drift = more languages.

Three Explanatory Factors

① Time

More generations = more drift (just modeled with Telephone Tree).

② Separation

Geographic AND social isolation. Surprise: the sea is often NOT a barrier — coastal communities across a strait can be more similar than communities on the same island.

③ Social Structure

Polynesian societies: large, hierarchical — holds one language together. Melanesian societies: small, egalitarian — harder to keep one language unified.

~5 min mini-lecture.
