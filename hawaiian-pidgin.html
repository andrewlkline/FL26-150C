What’s in the Name?

“Pidgin”: One Word, Two Meanings

In Hawaiʻi

  • “Pidgin” is spoken by an estimated 600,000 people in Hawaiʻi, and by an unknown number of former residents on the mainland
  • Short for “Pidgin English”, the name English speakers gave it. They saw it as a simplified or “broken” English
  • Most of its vocabulary comes from English, but it is a separate language

In linguistics

  • A pidgin is a new language that develops when speakers of different languages need to communicate but share no common language
  • Used in limited circumstances, learned as a second language
  • When it gains native speakers and a full range of uses, it becomes a creole

So: Pidgin in Hawaiʻi is, linguistically, a creole. Other names: Hawaiʻi Creole English (HCE), Hawaiʻi English Creole, Hawaiʻi Creole.

Sakoda & Siegel, pp. 150-151. The word “pidgin” is thought to come from the Chinese pronunciation of business. This slide ties directly to Tuesday’s definitions (Meyerhoff): pidgin = restricted domains, no native speakers; creole = everyday use, children acquire it from birth. Don’t spend long here; the activity right after makes the distinction concrete.

Sakoda & Siegel define a pidgin as used in limited circumstances, learned as a second language; a creole as a language that developed from a pidgin and now has a full range of functions, a complex grammar, and native speakers.

A Timeline of Pidgin

First Contact (before the plantations)

  • Polynesians settled the islands between about 200 and 400 AD; Captain Cook arrived in 1778 (native population estimated at 200,000 to 1,000,000)
  • Contact grew with the fur trade (1790-1810), sandalwood, and whaling. By 1848, only about 88,000 Hawaiians remained, mostly because of introduced disease
  • Traders and whalers learned some Hawaiian, and Hawaiians learned some English. No real pidgin yet, but features of other English pidgins were used
  • Still in Pidgin today: plenty (‘a lot’), by and by (future), no before a word to make it negative, and got/get (‘have’)

Callback (Oct 8): The chapter gives about 200-400 AD for settlement. Does that match the dates we saw for Hawaiʻi on Oct 8? Why might they differ?

Sakoda & Siegel, p. 152. Check this against the settlement dates on your Oct 8 slides; the chapter (2003) takes one position in a debate that has continued since, so newer estimates may be later. Features appear in Hawaiian sailors’ speech in Dana’s Two Years Before the Mast (1840), as discussed by Roberts (1998). Hana ‘work’ and pau ‘finished’ survive in pau hana.

The first sugar plantation opened in 1835. Laborers came from:

China (mostly Cantonese speakers) ~2,000 (1852-76); more than 37,000 (1877-97)

Portugal (Madeira and Azores) 10,000+ (1878-87); 13,000 more (1906-13)
Japan Began 1884; over 200,000 by 1924
Philippines Began 1907; over 100,000 by 1930
Others Korea (7,843), Puerto Rico (5,203), Russia (~3,000), Spain (~2,000), Germany (1,052), Norway and others (615)

Sakoda & Siegel, p. 153. Point out the Vanuatu connection: some of the same Pacific Islanders who ended up on Queensland plantations (Bislama’s origin) were also recruited to Hawaiʻi. Callback to last Tuesday: Meyerhoff says Hawaiʻi’s plantation pidgin survived for demographic and social-psychological reasons, because it became a marker of local identity.

The First Pidgin Was Hawaiian, Not English

  • In the early plantation era, Hawaiians still ruled their islands. Hawaiian was the language of government and of education for non-European children, so it became the language used to run the plantations
  • Many white overseers and Chinese laborers never learned it fully. A new form emerged with words mostly from Hawaiian, but different pronunciation, meanings, and structure. When Portuguese laborers arrived in the 1870s, it consolidated
  • So the first real pidgin in Hawaiʻi was Pidgin Hawaiian

How Pidgin Hawaiian differed from Hawaiian:

  • Dropped the ʻokina: piʻi mai became pi mai, meaning ‘come’ (in Hawaiian: ‘climb in this direction’)
  • Changed meanings: hapai ‘bring’ (from ‘carry’), makana ‘give’ (from ‘gift’)
  • Changed forms: hana hana ‘work’, nuinui ‘many’
  • Borrowed: pihi ‘fish’ (English fish), kaukau ‘food, eat’ (Chinese Pidgin English chow chow)

Sakoda & Siegel, p. 153. Callback to last Tuesday: Meyerhoff said pidgin Hawaiian faded as English took over, and Hawaiʻi’s pidgin kept going. This slide shows the sequence: Pidgin Hawaiian first, then Pidgin English. Don’t read every example; choose two or three.

Activity: Decode Pidgin Hawaiian (5 min)

In pairs:

  1. Translate it into natural English. (The court heard: Your horse went to eat my bran last night.)
  2. Compare with Hawaiian: Ua hele kou lio e ʻai i kaʻu palani i ka pō nei. What is missing in the pidgin? (Hint: ua)
  3. How is possession shown in each? (‘horse you’ vs. ‘your horse’)
  4. Which word order is more like English?

Example from Sakoda & Siegel, p. 153 (quoting Roberts 1995:41). Answers: (2) the pidgin lacks ua (marks completed events); (3) the pidgin puts the possessor after the thing possessed (lio oe ‘horse you’, palani wau ‘bran I’), whereas Hawaiian puts the possessive word before (kou lio, kaʻu palani); (4) the pidgin order, ‘horse your went’, differs from both Hawaiian (‘went your horse’) and English. Good moment to tie to Tuesday’s simplification and “function words dropped” from the pidgin Māori activity.

The Shift to English

  • 1875 Reciprocity Treaty with the US: free trade and more Americans. Hawaiians were fewer than 50,000 by 1878
  • 1878 to 1888: English-medium schools increased dramatically, Hawaiian-medium schools declined
  • Chinese and Portuguese families arrived (earlier, laborers were mostly single men), so more children were exposed to English in public schools
  • English gradually replaced Hawaiian on the plantations, and an English-lexified pidgin began to develop. At first, a mixture of Pidgin Hawaiian and English words:

Oe no tumach holoholo, hausu stop, mama nana. ‘You no too-much travel-around, house stop, mother look.’

  • New features: too much (‘a lot’ or ‘very’), stop (‘stay, live at’), me for ‘I’

Sakoda & Siegel, pp. 154-155. Quick optional prompt: which words in the example are Hawaiian (holoholo, nana) and which English (hausu, stop, mama)? Also include pilikia in the second example (Roberts 1993). Hawaiian words came into early Pidgin English, especially plantation words: hana hana, hapai ko ‘load cane’, hanawai ‘irrigate’, hoe-hana ‘hoe’. The chapter notes the language changed from the dominance of Hawaiian to English starting in 1875.

Pidgin English Becomes Established

  • Late 19th century: people started saying things in similar ways and a new structure emerged. Hawai’i Pidgin English (HPE) was established as a new auxiliary language
  • Early 1900s: HPE spread in mixed urban areas and public schools (most standard-English-speaking European children attended private schools)
  • Locally born Japanese children learned HPE from classmates; children learned it from school-age siblings and used it as a second language at home
  • But still learned as a second language, so speakers’ first languages shaped how they spoke it (e.g., Chinese speakers’ hully for ‘hurry’, dropped final sounds, allee for ‘all’)

Discuss: Which of Tuesday’s three clues for a pidgin (stable norms, restricted domains, variation from first languages) does HPE show? Which one is changing?

Sakoda & Siegel, pp. 155-156. The chapter’s example is a 1937 play (Bond) in which two older Chinese men saw wood: features like l for r (hully, licee), dropped final sounds (goo’, ligh’), -ee endings (allee, licee), and allee same ‘like’ and got at the end of a sentence, do not survive in modern Pidgin. Features that do survive: pake, no mo’, no can, kaukau, bymby pau, um for ‘him, her, it, them’, wahine. Use the optional play-sorting slide if you have time.

From Pidgin to Creole

  • Around 1900, the second generation of locally born Chinese and Portuguese appeared. Most parents were bilingual in their language and HPE, and many used HPE as their primary language
  • Parents spoke to newborns in HPE instead of Cantonese or Portuguese. Many Part-Hawaiian children also learned HPE as a first language, since many interethnic marriages used HPE at home
  • Now spoken as a first language, HPE was technically no longer a pidgin: Hawaiʻi Creole, modern Pidgin, began to emerge
  • Most linguists agree Pidgin was established as a distinct language between 1905 and 1920. Between 1920 and 1930, locally born children of immigrants equaled foreign born, and Pidgin became the language of the majority of Hawaiʻi’s population

Discuss: Compare with Bislama (Tuesday). What’s the same, and what’s different?

Sakoda & Siegel, p. 157. Link to Meyerhoff pp. 147-148: urbanization, intermarriage, children acquiring the language from birth. Differences: no institutional recognition as a national language; in Hawaiʻi, the creole emerged inside a context of English-medium schools and immigrant labor, with no single indigenous lingua franca. Bislama was named the national language after independence partly because it was neutral. HCE has never been recognized officially.

What Did the Creole Add?

Modern Pidgin has less variation in pronunciation than the earlier pidgin, and new grammar:

New in the creole Example
never for a past event that did not happen I never do like that.
stay for continuing action I stay working my house.
for meaning ‘to’ We are ready for play ball.

(Examples from a 1921 Hawaii Educational Review article that listed these as “incorrect” usage.)

Discuss: The 1921 article called these “incorrect.” Why do linguists disagree?

Sakoda & Siegel, pp. 157-158. The 1921 article also listed old pidgin features: I stop Honolulu, I no got pencil, Him blow my head, What for you talk like that?, I too much like that one, Suppose rain, I no can go, He look all same chicken. Many still survive. Key point: the chapter explicitly says linguists would not agree that structures in Pidgin or any other language are “incorrect” just because they differ from standard English. Tie to Jourdan & Angeli’s colonial hierarchy (Tuesday): “broken English” has the same logic.

Who Shaped Pidgin’s Grammar?

Three Substrate Languages

Hawaiian

  • Word order: Nui ka hale (‘big the house’) → Pidgin: Big, da house; Cute, da baby
  • Exclamations: Auwē, ka nani! (‘oh the pretty!’) → Oh, da pretty!

Cantonese

  • yáuh means both ‘have’ and ‘there is/are’ → Pidgin get: They get three sons. / Get one student he very bright.

Portuguese

  • para ‘for’ → fo/for: Charles is da man fo do ’um.
  • estar (location, state, progressive, result) → stay: Da book stay on top da table. Da water stay cold. John stay writing one letter. Da house stay pau already.

Sakoda & Siegel, pp. 159-160. Use this as the central “substrate” slide, tying to Tuesday: the languages speakers already know shape the creole’s grammar (compare Bislama’s inclusive/exclusive ‘we’ from Vanuatu languages). Also: the pronunciation of Pidgin has similarities to Hawaiian, Cantonese, and Portuguese, especially in vowel sounds and intonation in questions, but this has not been studied in detail.

  2. Da house stay pau already. (one word for several kinds of ‘be’)

Answers: 1 Cantonese (yáuh); 2 Portuguese (estar); 3 Hawaiian (Nui ka hale); 4 Portuguese (para); 5 Hawaiian (Auwē, ka nani!). This is a great live demonstration of the comparative method idea: the same sort of reasoning we used for cognates.

Why Portuguese More Than Japanese?

Japanese was the largest immigrant group, yet it influenced the grammar less. Why?

  • Timing: the first large Japanese immigration came in 1888, by which time Hawaiians, Chinese, and Portuguese had pretty well fixed the form of the pidgin (Reinecke 1969)
  • Locally born matter: it was locally born children who acquired the pidgin as a first language. Portuguese locally born equaled foreign born by 1900; Chinese just before 1920; Japanese later in the 1920s
  • Portuguese were the largest locally born immigrant group in 1896 (over half) and the largest immigrant group in schools (1881-1905, over 20% from 1890-1905)
  • Plantation power: Portuguese held many skilled and luna (‘foreman’) positions, three times as many as any other group
  • Language shift: the Portuguese were the first group to shift from their traditional language to Pidgin

But Japanese left a mark: about 40 words, translations like chicken skin ‘goose bumps’, discourse particles (yeah, no at the end of a sentence), and the structure of narratives.

Sakoda & Siegel, pp. 160-161. Discussion prompt: which factor matters most: numbers, timing, children, schools, or power? What does this say about how languages change? (Answer: the number of speakers is not enough; who is positioned to acquire the language as a child, and when, matters.) Sources cited: Roberts (2000), Siegel (2000), Masuda (2000), Reinecke (1969).

Pidgin Vocabulary

Words from Everywhere

Most Pidgin words come from English, but many have changed meaning or form, and many come from other languages:

English words, new meanings

  • pear ‘pear, avocado’
  • pipe ‘pipe, faucet’
  • shame ‘shame, shy’
  • off ‘turn off’ (off the light)
  • broke ‘broken, torn’

New compounds

  • talk story, stink eye, chicken skin, bolohead, bulai (bull + lie)

From other languages

  • Hawaiian (100+): akamai, kokua, ono, pau, pilikia, ʻohana, keiki
  • Japanese (~40): bento, shoyu, musubi, mochi, obake
  • Portuguese: malassada
  • Chinese: char siu, lihing mui
  • Filipino: adobo, bagoong
  • Korean: kimchee
  • Samoan: lavalava

Sakoda & Siegel, pp. 161-164. Other blended forms: haolefied, onolicious, hulihuli chicken, kalua pig, poi dog, chawan cut, daikon legs, buta kaukau. Point: Pidgin is not “English with errors”: it has its own meanings and word-building.

Attitudes and Pidgin Today

A Badge of Identity, and a Target

  • Pidgin has mostly been the informal language of families and friends, an important badge of local identity
  • Attitudes have always been ambivalent: important to local culture, but also called corrupted or “broken” English and seen as an obstacle to learning standard English, the language of schools, government, and big business
  • 1987: the Hawaiʻi Board of Education tried to adopt a policy allowing only standard English in schools. Parents, teachers, university faculty, and community groups reacted strongly; the policy was seen as discriminatory, and as an attack on Pidgin and local culture
  • Similar debates followed (1999, 2002), as some officials and members of the public blamed Pidgin for poor reading and writing test results

Compare with Tuesday (Honiara): Pijin was also called “broken English” and ranked below English. What do the two situations have in common? What’s different?

Sakoda & Siegel, p. 164 (see Sato 1989, 1991). The chapter was published in 2003, so all “recent” events are about 20+ years old. Connect to Jourdan & Angeli: social vs. linguistic legitimacy; hierarchical ideology; “Pijin spoelem English” vs. the claim that Pidgin hurts students’ standard English. Hawaiʻi’s difference: backlash from the community in 1987 was strong and public.

Pidgin Gains New Domains

  • Da Pidgin Coup (since 1998): a group mainly from UH Mānoa that meets regularly on linguistic, social, and educational issues about Pidgin. Their position paper, “Pidgin and Education,” gave research-backed information to officials and teachers
  • Print: Pidgin to da Max (1981) and its sequel sold more than 200,000 copies. Bamboo Ridge Press (1978) spurred local writing. Authors include Eric Chock, Lisa Kanae, Darrell Lum, Milton Murayama, Ed Sakamoto, Gary Pak, Lee Tonouchi, and Lois-Ann Yamanaka
  • Bible: Da Jesus Book (2000) sold over 11,000 copies in its first year

Heavy, Light, and In Between

Pidgin is not one fixed way of speaking:

Term What it means
Basilect (“heavy” or “strong” Pidgin) Very different from English
Mesolects Varieties in between; where most speakers are
Acrolect (“lighter” Pidgin) Close to standard English
  • Many speakers switch between heavier and lighter Pidgin depending on who they’re talking to, what they’re talking about, the setting, and the level of formality. Many are fully bilingual in Pidgin and standard English
  • A common belief: Pidgin is gradually becoming more like English. But the variation seems to have existed from the earliest days, and the wish to project a local identity will likely keep Pidgin distinct
  • There is no general agreement about what counts as Pidgin: for some, only the heavy basilect; for others, a local accent and some local words

Discuss: Where would you draw the line? Can someone “speak Pidgin” with an accent and a few words?

Sakoda & Siegel, p. 165. Shifting between heavier and lighter Pidgin is movement along a continuum. Connect to Tuesday’s Meyerhoff on dialect differences arising as children regularize variation.

New Research: Pidgin in the 21st Century

English Standard Schools

  • Public schools were English-medium by law (1896), yet in the 1920s white families newly arrived from the US lobbied for separate “English Standard” schools
  • Admission was by an oral English test. Children who failed, mostly Pidgin-speaking children of plantation workers, went to the regular schools
  • Phased out in the 1940s; the last class graduated in 1960
  • At UH Mānoa, teacher trainees were tested on English in the 1920s, and in 1943 freshmen who didn’t improve in required speech courses could be dismissed (speech courses stayed required into the 1970s)

Two effects

  1. Pidgin-speaking kids stayed together, which kept Pidgin alive
  2. Pidgin got tied to lesser education, and English to the white elite

Discuss: How is this different from what happened to Hawaiian in schools after 1896?

Higgins (2021), “Promoting Pidgin at UH Mānoa,” Ch. 10, pp. 1-3 (history section). Higgins quotes a 1920 Bureau of Education statement that white parents feared their children “would be outnumbered by the orientals,” so the issue was race and class as much as language. The link to Drager (2012): by the 1910s, Roberts (2004) finds English already associated with haole identity and Pidgin with being “local.” Discussion answer: Hawaiian-medium schools were closed down (nearly gone by 1896) and Hawaiian stopped being passed on in many homes; with Pidgin, schools kept Pidgin-speaking children together and, ironically, helped maintain it. Compare Tuesday’s colonial hierarchy in Honiara (English on top).

Pidgin vs. Hawaiʻi English

Most people in Hawaiʻi speak Pidgin, Hawaiʻi English, or both, and switch between them. Drager (2012) treats them as two languages on a continuum. Same lexifier, different grammar:

Feature Pidgin Hawaiʻi English
Past I wen go. (wen, or ben on Kauaʻi; optional if the time is clear) I went.
Negation We no eat dea. / We neva eat dea. (neva for past) We don’t / didn’t eat there.
Complements I wen go downtown fo eat lunch. (fo ← Portuguese para) …to eat lunch.
“Be” Da stew stei nice. (stei ← Portuguese estar; adds surprise) The stew is nice.
Adjective first Cute, da baby. (like Hawaiian ʻOno ka iʻa) The baby is cute.
  • In one study of fifth graders on Hawaiʻi Island, many had under 70% comprehension when listening to their non-dominant variety
  • Discourse particles (yeah, eh, no) show up in both, likely from Japanese

Discuss: Why do people say “broken English” about Pidgin, when the grammar here is so regular?

Drager (2012), “Pidgin and Hawaiʻi English: An overview,” pp. 61-73, sections 6-7; Reynolds (1999) for the comprehension study. Also Sakoda & Siegel (2012) in the Mouton World Atlas of Variation in English: preverbal markers for tense and aspect (stay habitual and progressive, bin and go for anterior and future) are typical of creoles; the use of stay shows the Portuguese substrate (estar). Drager’s footnote: linguists write “pidgin” for the contact variety and “Pidgin” for the creole. Mufwene’s “feature pool”: speakers draw from both. Connect to Tuesday: lexifier vs. substrate, and to the heavy-to-light slide (basilect, mesolect, acrolect). Hawaiʻi English also has its own history: Drager notes two meteorologists who sued the National Weather Service in 1987 over hiring on the basis of how they “sounded” (they were speaking Hawaiʻi English, not Pidgin).

Mapping Pidgin on Oʻahu

Drager & Grama (2014) gave people a blank map of Oʻahu and asked where people talk differently. They never mentioned Pidgin or English.

What they found

  • Maps followed the old moku (districts)
  • Waiʻanae and Waimānalo marked as Pidgin; Hawaiʻi Kai and Kāhala marked as English
  • Town: many languages (e.g., Ilocano, Samoan, Chinese)
  • Many maps added ethnicity, though the task asked only about language
  • Where Pidgin was named in “English” areas, it was a lighter Pidgin

Why it matters

  • People hold detailed, shared beliefs about place, ethnicity, and language
  • These beliefs are ideologies, like Jourdan & Angeli’s in Honiara

Activity (3 min): Sketch Oʻahu (or your home island). Where do you think people talk differently? What label would you give?

Drager & Grama (2014), Dialectologia 12, pp. 23-51. First perceptual dialectology study within Hawaiʻi. Table 2: 19 of 22 respondents who mentioned Waiʻanae labeled it Pidgin (86.4%). The study found that people believe language, ethnicity, and region are linked. Earlier work by Preston (1982, 1986) used a map of the US, where Hawaiʻi was just one spot labeled “Pidgin.” Caution for class: these are beliefs about speech, not measurements of speech; the authors note that further work should compare beliefs with actual variation. Invite students from other islands to share. Keep the discussion non-judgmental about neighborhoods.

Putting It Together

Stage What happened
Contact (1778-1830s) Traders and whalers; features of English pidgins; no real pidgin yet
Pidgin Hawaiian (1830s-1870s) Hawaiian dominant; plantations; words mostly Hawaiian
Pidgin English (1875-1900s) English replaces Hawaiian; new auxiliary language; too much, stop, no
Creole (1905-1930) Locally born children acquire it as a first language; new grammar (never, stay, for)
1920s-1960 English Standard schools sort children by spoken English
Today Marker of local identity; expanding into print; taught and studied at UH; debated in schools
